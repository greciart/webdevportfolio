Zeta Line is a Madrid company that designs, manufactures and installs office glass partitions and acoustic movable walls. Their clients are businesses that want to divide a floor without construction work, and hotels, schools or auditoriums that need to turn one large room into several smaller ones.

It's a technical product that's quoted to measure and decided slowly. Nobody buys a movable wall from their phone on a Tuesday afternoon. What does happen is that someone visits the site, compares options, forwards it to their architect and, if the site earned their trust, asks for a study. My job was to make that path work.

## The real problem

Zeta Line has six different systems: three partitions (Smart Glass, Twin Glass and Strong) and three movable walls (Maxparete, Domino and E-motion). For the people who make them, the differences are obvious. For an office manager who just wants to "close off the meeting room", they're six names that mean nothing.

And there was a second audience with the opposite needs: architects and interior designers. They don't need to be sold anything. They need specs, finishes and documentation to put the system into their project.

A site that talks to both the same way ends up convincing neither.

## How I solved it

**Two lines before six products.** The site is organized around the company's two product lines, which answer the question the client can actually answer: do you want a fixed division or one that moves? Partitions or movable walls. Each line has its own category page, and each system its own product page.

**Products explained by use, not by spec sheet.** Every page starts with what the system is for and what kind of space it fits. The technical details are there, lower down, for whoever is looking for them.

**A separate path for architects.** A technical documentation section with specifications, construction details and installation manuals, without going through the sales pitch.

**Applications as a shortcut.** Offices, meeting rooms, hotels, schools, auditoriums, restaurants, events and training centers. Many people don't know which system they need, but they do know what kind of space they have.

**Questions answered before contact.** The FAQ covers what anyone asks before requesting a quote: the difference between the three partition types, acoustic insulation, finishes, whether the layout can change later, and where the movable walls get installed.

**One conversion.** "Request a study" on every page, with a direct call and WhatsApp as alternatives. For a product that is always quoted to measure, the study is the first real step, so it's the only thing the site asks for.

## Architecture

Home, two category pages and six product pages, plus completed projects, contact and the legal pages. Any product is two clicks from the home page.

That structure isn't only for visitors. Each product page targets a specific search ("office partitions", "acoustic movable walls" or the system's own name) and each category page groups them. Google understands what each URL answers, and nobody lands on a generic page that makes them search again.

## Design

The client wanted something minimalist, clean and professional, with plenty of white space and an editorial approach. It fit the product: this industry sells with photography of finished spaces, glass and light, so the interface had to step back.

I translated that into white, greys, a single line of the brand blue and light typography, with a clear hierarchy and generous spacing. The photos of real projects do the work and the interface doesn't compete with them.

## SEO and performance

Here's the trap with a site like this: almost all the content is large photos. It's easy to end up with a beautiful site that takes five seconds to load.

<Image src={zetalinePagespeed} alt="PageSpeed Insights report for zetaline.es on desktop: 91 performance, 91 accessibility, 96 best practices and 100 SEO." widths={[480, 1005]} sizes="(max-width: 768px) 100vw, 768px" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

The desktop result, measured with PageSpeed Insights: **91 performance, 91 accessibility, 96 best practices and 100 SEO.** What went into the project:

- Optimized images, with descriptive alt text on the project photos
- Page titles, meta descriptions, headings and URLs built around one keyword per page
- Site copy written with those searches in mind, without stuffing
- **Google Search Console** set up and the sitemap submitted
- A **Google Business Profile** created, so the company shows up in local searches in Madrid

An honest note: that score is desktop. On mobile, a site this heavy on photography always scores lower, and that's the next thing to optimize.

## Stack

- **WordPress** with **Elementor Pro**
- On-page SEO per product: one page, one target search
- Google Search Console and Google Business Profile
- Contact by form, phone and **WhatsApp**

## Result

A site that separates the client who needs guidance from the architect who needs data, explains six technical systems without overwhelming anyone, and takes both to the same place: requesting a study. And it loads fast despite being, mostly, photography.

## Selling a technical product?

If your site lists everything you make but clients still call to ask the basics, the problem usually isn't the design. It's the order you tell things in. That's the first thing I look at.

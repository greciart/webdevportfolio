## The brief

CotizaGuate compares what Guatemalan retailers charge for the same electronics. The commercial problem was not the comparison engine, it was recognition: this is a category where every competitor looks like a spreadsheet with a logo on top, and nobody remembers a spreadsheet.

So the work started with identity rather than interface. Three pieces, usually split across three suppliers: the mark, a character, and the storefront the whole system would live on.

## Building the mark around a letter

The starting point was the **quetzal**, Guatemala's national bird and, conveniently, the name of the currency. Both meanings are in play here, which is exactly the kind of coincidence worth designing around.

Rather than setting the bird beside the name, I built it into the C of Cotiza, with circuit-board tracing forming the letterform. It reads as a monogram first and gives up the bird second.

<Image src={cgWordmark} alt="CotizaGuate logo lockup: quetzal built into a circuit-traced letter C, with the wordmark and tagline below and the three-colour palette at left." />

That decision came from where the mark actually has to survive. A comparison platform lives at 16 pixels, in favicons, app icons and partner listings. An illustrated bird turns to mush at that size. A letter with a silhouette inside it does not.

<Image src={cgPalette} alt="The CotizaGuate monogram on a black field beside its three colour swatches: primary teal, circuit blue and lime green." />

Three colours carry it: a teal drawn from the quetzal's plumage, a circuit blue, and a lime green for anything that needs to be pressed. The gold coin in the bird's beak does double duty as the currency reference and as the only warm note in an otherwise cool palette, so the eye lands on it.

<Image src={cgGlass} alt="Cut-glass render of the CotizaGuate monogram, showing how the mark holds its silhouette when rendered as a three-dimensional object." />

## Quetzo, the mascot

The character came next, and it needed a reason to exist beyond decoration. Price comparison is a dry errand. Someone arrives mildly irritated that they have to check eight tabs. A guide changes the register of that visit.

<Image src={cgSketches} alt="Pencil and ink concept sheet exploring the CotizaGuate mascot, from realistic bird studies to the stylised version perched on the letter C." />

Sketching came before any modelling. Silhouette, proportion and pose get resolved on paper in an afternoon; the same corrections cost days once a mesh exists. The sheet above tracks the move from an anatomical bird to a shape simple enough to read as a brand asset.

<Image src={cgMascot} alt="Final 3D render of Quetzo, the CotizaGuate mascot, perched on the illuminated monogram holding a gold coin in its beak." />

He is called **Quetzo**, a contraction of quetzal and Cotizo. Short, phonetic in Spanish, and it sounds like a piece of software. His written brief is deliberately narrow: analytical, quick, never wastes your time, and his one job is finding a lower price. Fur and feathers were modelled in **Blender**, with the circuit motif from the logo carried onto his wings so the two assets read as one system.

<Image src={cgSheet} alt="Quetzo character sheet with the mascot's name, personality description and technical profile, illustrated with the 3D render and a flat icon version." />

<Image src={cgTurnaround} alt="Quetzo turnaround with front, back and side views, plus eight poses covering walking, thinking, presenting and idle states." />

The turnaround and the pose set exist so the client is never stuck. A single hero render is a dead end the first time someone needs the character in a banner, an empty state or a push notification. Eight poses and three views mean the mascot can be deployed without another commission.

## Applying the system

<Image src={cgMerch} alt="Merchandise mockups applying the CotizaGuate identity to a laptop lid, phone case, smartwatch face, bottle, headphones and earbuds." />

Mockups are how you find out whether an identity actually works. Engraved on a laptop lid the mark loses its colour and has to survive on silhouette alone; on a watch face it drops to a few millimetres; on a phone case it gets the full palette. Testing across those extremes catches the failures a flat presentation board hides.

## The storefront

The site runs on **WordPress** with **WooCommerce**, built as a template written from scratch instead of a purchased theme bent into shape.

That came from a stated requirement: the client adds and edits products themselves, permanently, with no developer in the loop. A bought theme meets the visual brief faster and then resists every change after launch. Writing the template against the brand's own type, colour and spacing rules means the editing screens produce on-brand pages by default, rather than relying on anyone remembering the rules.

<Image src={cgFlow} alt="Wireflow for CotizaGuate mapping the route from search through category results and the comparison table to the affiliated retailer." />

The flow above was settled before any page was designed. It is short on purpose: search, compare, leave for the retailer. Every screen either narrows the options or hands the visitor off, and anything that did neither was cut.

Light and dark themes both ship, with the palette checked in each so prices stay legible either way. On a comparison platform, a figure that is hard to read is a failed page.

## Stack

- **Blender** for Quetzo, from block-out through fur, materials and final renders
- **Illustrator** for the monogram, wordmark and colour system
- **Photoshop** for the merchandise and packaging mockups
- **WordPress** with a hand-built template, no purchased theme
- **WooCommerce** for the product structure and retailer pricing data
- **Elementor** for the catalogue layouts the client maintains

## Outcome

A young platform that looks established, a mascot the client can deploy without commissioning artwork every time, and a catalogue they run themselves.

## Need the identity and the build from one place?

Splitting branding and development between two suppliers is where consistency tends to break. Handling both means the decisions made in the logo still hold by the time they reach the CMS.
